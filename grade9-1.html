Introduction:

Static electricity is the study of electric charges at rest. It explains how objects can attract or repel each other after being rubbed, touched, or brought close together. Static electricity plays an important role in daily life, technology, and natural phenomena such as lightning.

1.1 Atomic Structure

All matter is made of atoms. An atom consists of:

  • Protons (positive charge)
  • Neutrons (no charge)
  • Electrons (negative charge)

Protons and neutrons are located in the nucleus, while electrons move around the nucleus. Normally, an atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so it is electrically neutral. Static electricity occurs when electrons move from one object to another.

So an atom becomes negatively charged when it gains electrons and becomes positively charged when it loses electrons

What happens when an electron meets a proton?
When an electron meets a proton, they will create a electric force between them and The particles attract each other.

In short, charge directions:

  • Electrons → inwards
  • Protons → outwards

1.2 Electric charges

Charges can have different behaviors with each other:

  • Like charges repel each other. (Ex: Proton and Proton will repel each other)
  • Opposite charges attract each other. (Ex: Proton and Electron will attract each other)
  • The closer objects with like charges are to each other, the stronger it will repel.
  • The further objects with like charges are to each other, the weaker the repulsion will be.
  • The same applies to objects with unlike charges: The closer they are to each other, the stronger they will attract each other. The further they are from each other, the weaker the attraction will be.

The unit of electric charge is Coulomb (C).

1.3 Phenomena of Electrostatic

Polarization is the process of separating the charges within an object to two different sides while keeping the overall charge of the object neutral. If an object has gone through polarization, it is said to be polarized or in a state where the positive charge and the negative charges are separated like the two sides of a magnet.

Conductor: are materials that permit electrons to flow freely from particle to particle. An object made of a conducting material will permit charge to be transferred across the entire surface of the object.

In conductors (like metals):

  • Charges move freely.
  • Charges spread evenly on the surface.

Insulator: are materials that impede the free flow of electrons from atom to atom and molecule to molecule. The particles of the insulator do not permit the free flow of electrons; subsequently charge is seldom distributed evenly across the surface of an insulator.

In insulators (like plastic):

  • Charges cannot move easily.
  • Charges stay in one place.

1.4 Methods of Charging and Discharging

1) Charging by Rubbing (friction) (Triboelectric charging)

Triboelectric charging is a method of charging of which an object is rubbed on another object, causing the electrons to move from one object to another. (Example: Rubbing balloon against hair or wool)

This happens because of Electron Affinity. Electron affinity is a property of a material that describes the relative amount of affinity or love that its atoms have for electrons. Materials ranked higher in the lest will become negatively charged when rubbed with materials below them.

2) Charging by Induction

Induction charging is a method used to charge an object without actually touching the object to any other charged object. (Example: Buildup of dust in laptop screen→ Charging by Induction → Temporary)

3) Charging by Conduction

Charging by conduction involves contact, it is often called charging by contact.

4) Discharing by Grounding/Earthing

This happens when excess charge flows away, often through grounding (connecting to Earth). For example, touching a metal doorknob after walking on carpet can cause a small spark.

Earthing or Grounding → is the process of removing the excess charge on an object by means of the transfer of electrons between it and another object of substantial size. When a charged object is grounded, the excess charge is balanced by the transfer of electrons between the charged object and a ground.

1.5 Electric Charges Distribution

To measure the distribution of electric charge we can use an electroscope. it is a device used to detect electric charge. It can show whether an object is charged but not the exact amount.

It consists of:

  • A metal rod
  • A metal plate
  • Two thin metal leaves

When a charged object is brought near or touches it:

  • The leaves spread apart if charged.
  • The greater the charge, the wider they separate.

1.6 Coulomb’s Law of Electric Force and Electric Fields

Coulomb’s Law explains the force between two charges.

F = electric force
Q1 and Q2 = charges
R = distance between charges
K = Constant (9 × 10⁹)

Electric fields are areas surrounding a charged particle where other charges may experience a force.

  • Lines move from positive (+) to negative (-)
  • Closer lines → stronger fields
  • Field lines do not intersect or overlap
  • The force direction of a charged object placed in the electric field depends on the charge (positive or negative) and the field direction.
